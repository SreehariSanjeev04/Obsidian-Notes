    Analysis of the BODMAS Malware Dataset in Recent Research

    The BODMAS dataset was introduced by Yang et al. (IEEE SPW 2021) as a large, timestamped corpus of Windows PE binaries for malware analysis

    . It contains 57,293 malicious and 77,142 benign samples collected from Aug 2019 to Sept 2020, with carefully labeled family information (581 malware families, 14 high-level categories). Its design explicitly targets temporal analysis: samples are timestamped so that concept drift (changes over time) can be studied. The authors emphasize these strengths (recency and rich labeling) as key contributions. According to Google Scholar, the original BODMAS paper has been widely cited (≥147 citations)

    , reflecting its impact as a benchmark dataset.

    Despite these strengths, later work has noted limitations. For example, Zhou et al. (NDSS 2025) critique BODMAS as outdated, observing that it “provides only malware binaries from five years ago”

    (i.e. only through 2020). In practice, this motivates new, more up-to-date datasets. We summarize below how select top-tier works use or evaluate BODMAS, highlighting what each finds positive or problematic about the dataset.

    Key Papers Using or Analyzing BODMAS

    • Yang et al. (IEEE SPW 2021)

    – Original BODMAS dataset release (147 citations). The paper introduces BODMAS and describes its scope: ~57K malware, ~77K benign, timestamped (2019–2020), with well-curated family labels. Its contributions are explicitly the recency of samples and inclusion of detailed labels for both malware and benign files. (The benign binaries are provided via features due to licensing; the focus is on enabling learning-based malware analysis.) The authors perform preliminary concept-drift analyses using BODMAS, showing how classifier accuracy degrades over time. Overall, BODMAS’s strengths are its size, timestamps, and label richness, which have since made it a popular benchmark (noted by its high citation count- ). One limitation (noted indirectly by others) is its fixed time span – new variants beyond 2020 are not covered.

    • Chen et al. (USENIX Security 2023)

    – Active Learning for Malware Detection. This study evaluates active learning strategies for PE malware classification. It cites the BODMAS paper as prior work: “BODMAS… compared the following active learning sample selection schemes: random, uncertain, and non-conformity score” and found that uncertainty sampling performed best. In other words, Chen et al. use BODMAS to validate sampling methods: they leverage the BODMAS dataset (labels from 2019–2020) to compare how different query strategies affect learning. This work thus confirms and extends BODMAS’s value in studying retraining: the authors note that BODMAS showed uncertainty-based sampling to be very effective- . They do not criticize BODMAS explicitly, but their use of it underlines its role as a modern PE malware benchmark.

    • Gibert et al. (CCS 2023, Workshop)

    – Certified Robustness against Patch/Appends. This workshop paper adapts ideas from randomized smoothing to malware. Crucially, the authors train and test on BODMAS: they “trained a classifier with our chunk-based scheme on the BODMAS dataset” and demonstrate that their “chunk-based smoothed classifier is more robust against…evasion attacks” than a standard model- . In other words, BODMAS is used as the benchmark for evaluating a novel defense. The authors implicitly confirm BODMAS’s adequacy by using it for training. They do not critique the dataset, but their results show that even on BODMAS’s relatively recent samples, robustness against attacks is a key concern.

    • Zhou et al. (NDSS 2025)

    – Concept-Drift Adaptation in Windows Malware. In this forthcoming NDSS paper, the authors build a new dataset spanning 2024 to study drift. They contrast their data with existing ones, noting: “The BODMAS dataset… provides only malware binaries from five years ago”

    • . This is a direct limitation: since BODMAS stops at 2020, it lacks current malware families. Zhou et al. highlight this gap as motivation for a new dataset. They also note that BODMAS (being Windows-focused) and another dataset (EMBER) are outdated, and thus their MB-24 dataset (2024 malware) offers a more realistic testbed. Thus, this work explicitly criticizes BODMAS’s recency, using it as an example of older research data that must be refreshed.

    1. Usage in Experiments: The authors include BODMAS as one of two Windows PE datasets

    . BODMAS files (2,381 static features each) span October 2019–September 2020. They use October–December 2019 data for training, January–March 2020 for validation, and April–September 2020 for testing. Hyperparameters are tuned on BODMAS and then applied to the EMBER dataset. Beyond binary malware detection, BODMAS is also used for an 11-class malware-family task (top-10 families + benign). In that multiclass setting (with the same temporal splits), XGBoost on BODMAS achieves ~71% F1 (improving slightly to ~71.4% with ADAPT). The dataset is likewise used in an active-learning scenario (50 samples/month), where ADAPT+AL boosts BODMAS F1 by about 3% over a random sampling baseline- .

    • Pros/Strengths: According to the paper, BODMAS is a well-structured, real-world Windows malware corpus with consistent static features

    . It yields very high baseline detection accuracy: for example, a vanilla XGBoost model already achieves ~99.2% F1 on BODMAS. This indicates that BODMAS’s classes are well-separated and its distribution is relatively stable over time. ADAPT (the proposed method) further marginally improves this, raising XGBoost’s F1 by ~0.4% while also reducing both false positives and false negatives. In other words, BODMAS’s stability allows tree-based models to perform extremely well, and ADAPT simply maintains or slightly enhances that high performance across the test period. In the active-learning experiment, ADAPT’s integration improves BODMAS results (e.g. +3% F1)- , again reflecting the dataset’s amenability to semi-supervised tuning.

    • Cons/Limitations: The paper notes that BODMAS exhibits very little concept drift (“less pronounced effect”)

    . While this makes BODMAS easy for the models (hence the ~99% F1), it also limits the dataset’s challenge for drift-robust methods. In fact, none of the self-training baselines beats the plain XGBoost on BODMAS, so adaptation yields only a tiny gain (0.4% F1). Thus, BODMAS’s limited drift means there’s little room to demonstrate ADAPT’s benefit – a fact the authors highlight. In the multiclass setting, the task is tougher (baseline F1 ~71%), but even there ADAPT’s improvements are modest. In summary, the authors imply that BODMAS’s very high baseline accuracy (and imbalanced/malware-heavy composition) reflects a dataset with minimal drift; this makes it less revealing for evaluating concept-drift adaptation and constrains the observable gains from advanced methods

    Here’s a concise summary of what this paper alone (RAID 2025 – ADAPT) tells us about the BODMAS dataset, independent of the ADAPT method.

    Observed Properties of BODMAS

    The BODMAS dataset is a large Windows PE malware collection (57,293 malicious vs. 77,142 benign samples

    ) covering August 2019–September 2020. Each sample is represented by a standard 2381-dimensional static feature vector (Ember format). It includes rich metadata: timestamps (chronologically sorted) and expert-curated family labels (581 malware families). These features yield very high separability – for example, classifiers on BODMAS typically achieve ≈97–99% F1 after periodic retraining. In practice, the paper notes only a modest degradation over time: concept drift exists but is mild (with retraining F1 remaining above ~96%

    ).

    Practical Strengths

    • Large, Up-to-date Benchmark. BODMAS’s size and recency (data from 2019–2020) make it a rigorous testbed for modern malware detection

    • .

    • Consistent Feature Space. Using the Ember/LIEF feature format (2381 dims)

    • means models can leverage well-established static features consistently across all samples.

    • Rich Labels. Having timestamp and family labels enables temporal and family-based analyses; it supports both binary detection and multi-class attribution tasks.

    • Balanced Diversity. BODMAS contains many benign and malware samples, so it approximates a realistic benign:malware ratio and helps benchmark classifiers at scale.

    • High Baseline Performance. The strong class separability (e.g. ~97–99% F1 with retraining

    • ) means that even simple models perform very well, making BODMAS useful as a “solved” reference to compare new techniques.

    Limitations

    • Moderate Concept Drift. Because the dataset spans only one year with steadily accumulating new families, the implied drift is relatively low. The original analysis showed only slight performance drop on newer samples

    • . In ADAPT’s context, this suggests less room for improvement from drift-adaptation techniques on BODMAS.

    • Class Imbalance. BODMAS has more benign than malware samples (∼1.35:1 ratio

    • ). While not extreme, this mild imbalance can bias learning and is noted by the authors in related work.

    • Homogeneous Data Domain. All samples are Windows executables with static features; there are no dynamic or network behaviors. Thus BODMAS does not capture cross-platform or behavioral aspects of malware.

    • Labeling Restrictions. (As noted by the dataset providers) benign binaries are not freely shared, limiting some forms of analysis.

    • Time Span. The one-year window may omit longer-term trends. BODMAS focuses on recent malware, so it may miss older or future-scope drifts.

    In summary, the ADAPT paper’s use of BODMAS suggests a dataset that is large, up-to-date, and “clean” (well-labeled with little noise), leading to very high baseline accuracy. These traits make BODMAS a valuable benchmark

    , but also imply limited concept drift challenge and some benign skew, which one must account for in evaluation

    .

    Sources: Dataset summary and analysis from the BODMAS publication and documentation

    (as reflected by the ADAPT paper’s experimental context).
